*Triad 23 of 32 · 2026-10-16 · Difficulty 3 · Agreement, Conflict, Shared figure or motif*

## The thread

Three arguments, six centuries apart, against the polished, the bright and the complete: Kenkō wants 'things with an air of age' and says 'It is incompleteness that is desirable'; Ruskin locates a building's glory 'in its Age' and calls restoration 'a Lie'; Tanizaki defends tarnished silver and soot-darkened rooms, then admits the 'sheen of antiquity' is 'just dirt.' They split over touch: Tanizaki's sheen is made by 'the caress of a hand in the same place'; Ruskin's stones are sacred because 'They are not ours'; Kenkō's palace keeps 'an unfinished place' by design. Tanizaki's maid, scolded for polishing a silver bowl just as it gained its patina, is all three positions at once. Secondary motif: the lamp, from Kenkō's lamplight to Ruskin's metaphor to Tanizaki's obsolete oil lamps.

## The three books

- [[books/1155-in-praise-of-shadows|In Praise of Shadows]], [[people/jun-ichiro-tanizaki|Jun'ichirō Tanizaki]], [[people/gregory-starr|Gregory Starr]] (translator); 1933. *Anchor: makes the value of time's mark concrete, then undercuts it ('just dirt'); the only one of the three for whom the mark comes from use and touch.*
- [[books/2833-essays-in-idleness|Essays in Idleness]], [[people/yoshida-kenko|Yoshida Kenkō]], [[people/g-b-sansom|G. B. Sansom]] (translator); c. 1330–32. *Ancestor and hinge: the native source of Tanizaki's taste for the worn and incomplete, and the Buddhist position from which Ruskin's anxious preservation looks like attachment.*
- [[books/4017-the-lamp-of-memory|The Lamp of Memory]], [[people/john-ruskin|John Ruskin]]; 1849. *Western, moral counterpart: age as the glory of a building and the 'golden stain of time'; the sharpest opposite to Tanizaki's handled sheen, a prohibition on touching.*

## Notebook

1. Tanizaki's sheen comes from 'the caress of a hand in the same place' (p. 32); Ruskin's stones are sacred because 'They are not ours' (¶13). Take one object you own that carries the marks of your hands and write which regime it lives under, and who besides you would be entitled to polish it.
2. Kenkō's palace keeps 'an unfinished place' by design (§82); Ruskin calls a restoration that fakes the old 'a Lie' (¶12). Is a ruin built in at the start a lie in Ruskin's sense, or the only honest building? Try it on a text: the deliberate lacuna, the fragment published as fragment.
3. Tanizaki's regress of elders (p. 78) is Kenkō's §22 restated. Write the lament your successor in 2126 will write about 2026: the thing they'll miss that you now find too bright.
