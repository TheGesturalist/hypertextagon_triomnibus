## The thread

Three books about a promise that outlives the person who made it. Kadare's Kostandin gave his mother his besa to bring Doruntine home. He dies, his mother curses him at the grave, and the dead man rides back. Agamben argues the curse is not added to the oath but co-originary with it, two outcomes of one act in which the speaker stakes himself on his word. Frayling's anthology carries the ballad's literary descendants (Bürger's 'Lenore' → 'the dead travel fast' in Dracula) and shows that the English vampire tale opens with a dying man extracting an oath (Byron's 'Fragment', 1816; Polidori, 1819). The oath runs in opposite directions: in Kadare it brings the dead back to deliver someone; in Polidori, 'Remember your oath' silences the living while the dead returns to take someone. In both, the curse half does the work: the mother's malediction, and the self-curse in Tolstoy's 'Vourdalak' ballad ('If I should break my oath, come to me after your death'). Agamben: 'It is from the oath—or, better, from perjury—that magic and spells are born.'

- [[books/4593-vampyres-genesis-and-resurrection-from-count-dracu|Vampyres: Genesis and Resurrection, from Count Dracula to Vampirella]], [[people/christopher-frayling|Christopher Frayling]] (editor); 1978 (rev. 1991; edition 2016). *Genealogy and inversion: Lenore → 'the dead travel fast'; the vampire tale opens with an extracted oath that silences the living*

## Notebook

1. Kadare ch. 6 (besa 'delicate, like a wild flower'; external laws shed 'as a snake sloughs off its old skin') vs Agamben p. 345 (law and religion bind 'by means of curses and anathemas'): can an oath be internal, with no curse attached, or does the mother's curse (ch. 2) show even Kostandin's besa needed one?
2. Polidori's 'Remember your oath' vs Stres's 'it was all of us … who, through Kostandin, brought Doruntine back' (ch. 7): once the swearer is dead, who holds the oath? Write a page in which the oath is the only surviving party, and it speaks.
3. Sdenka's ballad in 'Vourdalak' ('If I should break my oath, come to me after your death and drink all my heart's blood!') next to Agamben p. 333 ('It is from the oath—or, better, from perjury—that magic and spells are born'): draft a ballad stanza in which the conditional clause is the spell.
