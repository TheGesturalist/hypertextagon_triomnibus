## The thread

Who may teach, and who may be taught? Rancière (The Ignorant Schoolmaster) recovers Jacotot, who from 1818 taught what he did not know and held that 'the same intelligence is at work in all the productions of the human mind' (p. 18). Steiner (Lessons of the Masters) defends the asymmetry of Master and disciple. Williams (Self-Taught) documents enslaved and freed people learning and teaching without masters, under antiliteracy statutes passed 1819–1834, the same years Jacotot's news was 'proclaimed in Saint Petersburg and New Orleans' (p. 18). Hinge: Meno's slave boy. For Rancière, Socrates 'interrogates a slave who is destined to remain one' (p. 29); for Steiner, whoever grasps Socrates 'is made an autodidact' (ch. 1), and 'the very word "Master" carries the stain of slavery' (ch. 5). Conflict + same period + oblique/structural.

## The three books

- [[books/8939-the-ignorant-schoolmaster-five-lessons-in-intellec|The Ignorant Schoolmaster: Five Lessons in Intellectual Emancipation]], [[people/jacques-ranciere|Jacques Rancière]], [[people/kristin-ross|Kristin Ross]] (translator); 1987. *Anchor: the case for the ignorant master and the equality of intelligences.*
- [[books/3587-lessons-of-the-masters|Lessons of the Masters]], [[people/george-steiner|George Steiner]]; 2003. *Opponent: the eloquent defense of mastery and discipleship.*
- [[books/12076-self-taught-african-american-education-in-slavery|Self-Taught: African American Education in Slavery and Freedom]], [[people/heather-andrea-williams|Heather Andrea Williams]]; 2005. *Ground: learning and teaching without masters, under law that forbade both.*
