*Triad 19 of 32 · 2026-10-12 · Difficulty 4 · Shared form or method, Oblique/structural, Conflict*

## The thread

Galen's market theory of forgery ('when those, who offered the relics of some old writer, were rewarded, many works appeared with false titles', quoted by Lange, In the Second Degree p. 3) tested across three forgeries: the Seneca–Paul letters (Smolak), Chatterton's Rowley written into the gaps of Barrett's Bristol history and Walpole's Anecdotes of Painting (Taylor, pp. 52, 67), and 'Araki Yasusada' supplying the Hiroshima-witness voice American journals wanted (Perloff's afterword). Structural link: each forgery travels with an apparatus that is both alibi and tell — Paul's bad Latin 'blamed on the translator' (Smolak p. 228), Chatterton's Anglo-Saxon glossed for Walpole from the A entries of one dictionary (p. 67), Yasusada's editors' note that he 'could only have had the original French version' of Empire of Signs in a 1967 letter (p. 79), though L'Empire des signes appeared in 1970. Conflict: Lange, Taylor and Johnson all resist the word 'forgery'.

## Notebook

1. Galen against Lange: Galen says forgeries follow the money; Lange (p. 9) says ancient attributions 'root the origins of the cultural heritage in question in the mythical beginnings of culture.' Place Chatterton's 'Ryse of Peyncteynge' (Taylor p. 67) and the APR supplement (Perloff p. 154) on that scale.
2. Write the apparatus first: Chatterton ruled his manuscript so the notes would dominate (Taylor p. 63); Yasusada's editors take ~23% of the words. Draft three footnotes for a text that doesn't exist, then write the text they require.
3. The translator as alibi: Smolak p. 228 ('could be blamed on the translator'); Yasusada's three translators; Chatterton's self-glossing. What does the translator figure license that an author cannot claim? Test against Benjamin's 'Task of the Translator'.
