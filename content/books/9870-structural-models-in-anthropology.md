## Role in the triad

Formal ground: exact definitions (cutpoint, bridge, hamiltonian, eulerian) and the claim that graph theory is already in anthropology.

## Why this book

Gives the vocabulary Moretti uses by eye and the best parallel for Perec's hidden structure: New Hebrides sand drawings, which practise Euler's theorem (one line, finger never lifted) without stating it. Insists the mathematics must be made explicit.

## Quotations

*Verified against the book's own text on the day of the reading.*

- “But classification according to structure is generally more important. And it is the same with ideas.” (epigraph, Peirce)
- “In theory the whole should be done in a single continuous line which ends where it began; the finger should never be lifted from the ground, nor should any part of the line be traversed twice” (p. 10, quoting Deacon 1934)
- “whatever the particular configurations may look like or represent, they are eulerian; that is, they are connected and all points have even degree.” (p. 12)
- “Euler replaced each land area by a point and each bridge by a line joining the corresponding points, thereby producing the first published graph theoretic model.” (p. 12)
- “graph theory is such a congenial mode of anthropological thought that if Euler had not discovered it, some anthropologist might very well have.” (p. 13)
- “A cutpoint of a connected graph is one whose removal (together with its incident lines) disconnects it” (p. 19)
- “If a graph G has a spanning cycle Z, then G is called a hamiltonian graph and Z is a hamiltonian cycle.” (p. 26)
- “the individuals and relations represented by cutpoints and bridges would presumably be of fundamental importance in the group.” (p. 29)
